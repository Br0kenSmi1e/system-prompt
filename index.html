System Prompt
Is All You Need?

September 2026 · Draft

I was using sci-brain’s autoresearch workflow to look for new algorithms. It kept failing.

But failed experiments weren’t really what bothered me. Research fails all the time. What bothered me was how mechanical the whole thing felt.

The agent implemented ideas, ran benchmarks, wrote reports, and handed the work back. When we first audited the campaign, it had completed ten standalone attempts and 360 valid runs. None beat the stronger baseline. The machinery was working. I wasn’t getting much closer to an algorithm I cared about.

I put it this way in the conversation:

“i feel like the agent is just following the skill mechanically. to me, it just wants to get the work done to throw back something to me, rather than being a curious researcher.”

That became the start of a much longer experiment: not just searching for algorithms, but figuring out how I wanted an agent to search.

Why is the research still my job?

For context, I work on tensor-network contraction order. You can evaluate the same network in different orders, and the amount of computation can change dramatically. I wanted a new algorithm for finding good orders—not just a faster implementation of an old one.

The agent’s first response to my frustration was another set of workflow recommendations. More discriminating questions. Better diagnostics. More careful handling of negative results. Reasonable advice, but it already felt like we were adding another checklist.

Then something happened inside this very conversation that explained my frustration better than any benchmark.

I asked the agent to survey how other people build AI research systems. It gave me four possible survey angles and ended with:

Next: reply “all four,” or name the angles you want prioritized.

I had asked for findings. It gave me homework.

We traced that response to a survey-skill instruction requiring a strategy-selection menu. The agent was following the procedure, but the procedure put a decision back on me that I had intended to delegate.

I didn’t know which direction would be most interesting. That was why I wanted a survey. As I told it:

“in research, i also do not know the answer, otherwise, this is not a ‘research’.”

That was the real problem. I could delegate execution, but the mental work of deciding what to investigate kept coming back to me.

Were the skills “toxic”? I actually asked that. The history didn’t support such a simple answer. There were connection failures, mistakes in scientific judgment, and even stops that contradicted the skill’s own instruction to continue. Still, the survey menu was a concrete example of a rule getting in the way.

What if I just let it think?

I liked the minimalism of pi. Looking at it alongside Karpathy’s autoresearch and pi-autoresearch, I started wondering whether I needed such an elaborate research workflow at all.

My wording at the time was “free agent’s mind.” I believed Astra had the ability to research. Maybe I should give it a clear objective, useful tools, and room to decide what came next.

So we tried a clean setup: replace the system prompt, keep ordinary file and shell tools, add web access for reading, and let the agent investigate. I wanted to start normal pi, not launch a separate research platform.

Even getting there took some pushing back. The setup initially grew a launcher, an isolated runtime, and more Markdown files than I wanted. I asked for a small .pi configuration instead. Global skills were still loading at one point; a duplicate web extension broke startup at another. “Minimal” took some cleanup.

The arrangement we settled on was easy to understand:

.pi/SYSTEM.md      How to approach the research
.pi/settings.json  Model and tool configuration
RESEARCH.md        The objective and what we have learned
.worktrees/        Separate experiment workspaces

I kept the worktree idea from sci-brain. I also cared a lot about research memory. I didn’t want the agent to start from zero each time, or to mistake an old recommendation for a permanent instruction. The notebook should explain what we had learned, what had failed, and what was still uncertain.

Whenever the proposed prompt grew too detailed, I pushed back:

“too long, this is not a prompt that a human would write”

I wasn’t trying to replace a long skill with an equally long system prompt. I wanted something short enough to read and argue about.

The agent could work. But did it have taste?

The minimal setup didn’t magically produce the research I wanted.

One session found engineering speedups. Another produced a careful study of how preprocessing could exclude good contraction orders. There were proofs, reproducible experiments, and sensible caveats. The reports were not empty.

They still weren’t what I was asking for.

I wanted a competitive new algorithm with an idea worth understanding. An implementation speedup or a small diagnostic could help get there, but I didn’t want either one treated as the destination. At one point I described the agent as having bad “taste.”

That is an awkward thing to put into a prompt. “Do more interesting research” tells the agent almost nothing. We had to make the distinction more concrete.

The objective went into RESEARCH.md: develop a substantively new contraction-order algorithm, minimize total contraction work, and don’t turn the problem into a space-constrained or read/write-weighted optimization task. We also made it explicit that diagnostics and faster implementations were supporting work, not the objective.

The system prompt got a different instruction:

Before substantial implementation, state the proposed algorithmic advance, why it matters, its assumptions, and expected scaling.

I wanted the agent to notice the gap between “I can implement this” and “this is worth investigating” before spending the session on it.

We kept saving the experiments, restoring a clean starting point, and trying again. That let us inspect what happened without erasing the history. It also revealed another complication: a fresh conversation wasn’t necessarily a fresh start. The agent could find old worktrees and inherit their research direction.

A list of ideas is not an open investigation.

The next pattern was subtler. The agent could list several directions, choose one, and investigate it quite intelligently. But the alternatives mostly stayed on the list.

I wanted something more active: investigate a few possibilities, learn enough to compare them, improve them, combine them, and let the investigation generate new possibilities. Narrow the search progressively, rather than picking a favorite after the opening brainstorm.

We spent a surprising amount of the conversation trying to express this. “Start with several ideas” wasn’t enough. Neither was “reconsider alternatives when the favorite fails.” I wanted the possibilities to stay open even while something was working.

The distinction finally became clearer when I said we should be welcoming about adding ideas, strict about killing them, and active about modifying them.

A bad first implementation should not kill an entire direction. Setting an idea aside should not quietly become a claim that it cannot work. And keeping an idea alive does not mean giving everything equal time.

This became one of the central guidelines:

Maintain an evolving pool of distinct ideas there. Welcome new directions and actively revise or combine ideas as you learn. Progressively compare and deepen promising candidates without committing prematurely.

The rest of that guideline asks for strong evidence before rejecting an idea. The full prompt is short enough to read in one sitting.

There were encouraging moments. A survey-first session moved away from the previous line of work and proposed a symmetry-based exact planner. That was a genuinely different direction, not just another implementation tweak. Its reported advantage was narrow, and novelty was still unresolved, but it was closer to the kind of work I wanted.

Then it finished the study and stopped.

We had asked it to “keep going,” but also to “report a substantive result or a genuine blocker.” Looking back, that offered two different interpretations of when the job was done. I wanted a result to change the next research decision, not automatically end the session.

The next proposed correction was simple:

Keep this result as a checkpoint, not the finish line.
Reassess and evolve the idea pool, then continue investigating.

That was a correction to try, not a solved stopping problem. And there is a practical limit: when the model ends its turn, this pi setup does not automatically start another one. A sentence in the prompt is not a continuation mechanism.

So, is a system prompt all you need?

I don’t know yet. This did not end with a breakthrough algorithm or a completely autonomous researcher.

But I found the smaller setup easier to reason about. When the agent did something I disliked, I could inspect the instructions and the actual conversation, then change a specific expectation. Was the objective unclear? Was I accidentally asking for a report rather than continued investigation? Did “compare ideas” mean once at the beginning, or throughout the work?

The point wasn’t to remove every constraint. I still wanted honest evidence, sensible resource use, isolated experiments, and useful memory. I wanted to stop prescribing the investigative path in so much detail.

Skills can still be useful for a particular job. This experience just made me less eager to turn the whole research process into one. Nor did moving instructions into a system prompt prove that they work better there—we changed too many things along the way to claim that.

What I was really tuning was the amount of judgment I was willing to delegate, and how clearly I could tell the agent what mattered.

I don’t want to decide every next step and have the agent execute it. I want to give it a problem I don’t know how to solve—and have it help me figure out where to go.

A side experiment that didn’t settle anything

We also tried calling the agent a “coding agent,” an “algorithm researcher,” or giving it no identity sentence. Each got five minutes on the same task, using gpt-6-astra with low reasoning. We did one complete batch with research guidelines and another without them.

All six runs reached the time cap, with no obvious qualitative separation between identities. That didn’t tell us enough to answer the question. It certainly didn’t explain the broader experience described here.

Two interrupted batches came before the completed no-guidelines batch: one contained an extra five-minute run followed by a WebSocket failure; another failed before any tool calls. They are retained in the attempt metadata. The input texts and notes remain available, but this was a small demo, not a benchmark.

System Prompt
Is All You Need?

September 2026 · Draft

I was using sci-brain’s autoresearch workflow to look for new algorithms. It kept failing.

Failed experiments weren’t really what bothered me. Research fails all the time. What bothered me was how mechanical the whole thing felt: implement an idea, run the evaluation, write a report, hand the work back.

The agent was doing the tasks. I was still responsible for noticing what mattered and deciding where the research should go.

I put it this way:

“i feel like the agent is just following the skill mechanically. to me, it just wants to get the work done to throw back something to me, rather than being a curious researcher.”

That made me wonder whether the workflow was helping the agent investigate—or giving it a procedure to finish instead.

Why is the research still my job?

The problem became especially clear when I asked the agent to look into other AI research systems. Here’s my request and its reply. My message is unchanged; […] marks omitted text in the reply.

Me

could you do a survey? i believe a lot people have tried to use ai for research, how do they design the framework?

Agent

[…]

I recommend four angles:

  1. Framework landscape: minimal agents, fixed pipelines, multi-agent teams, and evolutionary search.
  2. Research memory: how systems retain evidence, failed attempts, hypotheses, and evolving understanding.
  3. Negative results: where agents become repetitive, produce unsupported claims, or need human intervention.
  4. Evaluation: whether reported gains demonstrate scientific discovery, benchmark optimization, or merely task completion.

[…]

Next: reply “all four,” or name the angles you want prioritized.

I had asked for findings. It gave me homework.

Those were reasonable search angles. The problem was making me choose before it did anything. I didn’t know which would be interesting. Finding that out was part of the work I wanted to delegate.

We traced the menu to an explicit instruction in the survey skill. In this case, the agent was following the rule—and that was exactly the problem. The workflow assigned a research decision to me that I wanted the agent to make.

This didn’t explain every failure in the earlier campaign. But it gave me a concrete reason to question the design: how many of our rules were helping the investigation, and how many were just making it wait for me?

What if I just let it think?

I liked the minimal design of pi. It made me wonder whether I could get the research behavior I wanted by changing the system prompt instead of building another elaborate skill.

My wording at the time was “free agent’s mind.” I believed Astra had the ability to research. Maybe I should give it the objective and the tools, then leave it room to decide whether to read, derive, code, experiment, or reconsider an assumption.

So we tried it. We replaced the procedural research workflow with a system prompt describing how to approach the problem. We kept a notebook, RESEARCH.md, for the objective and the understanding accumulated along the way.

The important change wasn’t the number of words. It was what the instructions tried to control: what the agent should care about, rather than which step it should perform next.

For this setup, the minimal system prompt solved the mechanical-workflow problem. The agent could choose what to read, implement, and test without making me manage a prescribed sequence of steps.

But that exposed a different problem. It had more freedom to investigate—and still converged too early.

The new problem: premature convergence.

My research problem was tensor-network contraction order: finding an order of operations that makes a large calculation cheaper. I wanted a competitive new algorithm, not just a faster implementation or a diagnosis of why an existing method fails.

The agent was now doing real investigative work. It developed ideas, checked them against small exact solutions, corrected mistakes, and ran comparisons. But it tended to settle on one direction quickly, work until it had a modest result, and report back.

There were two kinds of “premature” here. Premature commitment: choosing an approach before investigating the alternatives enough. And premature stopping: treating a completed study as a completed research task.

In one later run, it developed a method for combining parts of different contraction trees. There was an interesting idea, but the measured advantage was small and its broader value remained unclear. The final report even named a concrete next investigation. No blocker was reported. It still stopped.

This was different from the earlier approval menus. The agent wasn’t waiting for permission to follow the next prescribed step. It was making its own decisions—but deciding too soon that it had done enough.

An idea pool didn’t prevent it.

We had already tried to address early commitment with an evolving pool of ideas: welcome new possibilities, actively revise them, and demand strong evidence before ruling them out. The point was to let investigation change where the effort went.

Yet the agent could maintain that pool and still spend nearly all its effort within one family of methods. In the follow-up discussion, I asked:

“why it always focus on dp?”

DP means dynamic programming: solving a problem by combining solutions to smaller subproblems. The agent had explored symmetry-based DP, ways to combine contraction trees using DP, and then ways to detach and reattach fragments using DP.

These weren’t identical algorithms. There was genuine learning between them. But each new direction seemed to become another variation of the same basic architecture. Even an idea about choosing useful cuts became a way to feed a DP calculation.

It wasn’t explicitly refusing other architectures. It kept translating new possibilities into the architecture it was already using.

That is a subtler failure than having no alternatives. The notebook could look open-minded while the actual experiments stayed narrow.

DP might still be the right choice. I didn’t want to ban it or force the agent to switch for the sake of variety. I wanted evidence that made the choice worth committing to.

The agent could revise a result: it later downgraded an earlier small gain as unstable after a timing-sensitive repeat. But reconsidering the performance claim didn’t necessarily lead it to reconsider the research direction.

Explore the uncertainty, not just the implementation.

The pattern reminded me of a useful engineering habit: choose an architecture, diagnose its weaknesses, and keep refining it until it works.

But in this research, whether that architecture was worth pursuing was itself an open question. Improving the implementation could leave that question almost untouched.

That changed how I thought about the idea-pool guideline. A list of possibilities wasn’t the goal. I wanted the agent to choose investigations whose outcomes could change its judgment.

If a prototype performs poorly, is the mechanism weak, the implementation inadequate, or the test unsuitable? If it improves slightly, does that support the proposed idea—or did it simply get more search effort? What would make us deepen this direction, and what would make us investigate something else?

We discussed replacing the emphasis on maintaining a pool with a more direct principle:

Choose investigations that resolve consequential uncertainties and can change which approach you pursue. Treat the current architecture as a hypothesis, not a commitment; an idea pool matters only when evidence changes what you investigate.

This also changes what should happen after a result. A finished experiment gives the agent evidence for its next decision. It needn’t be the point where the whole investigation ends.

The frustrating part is that we can explain this clearly in conversation. Getting the working agent to act on it consistently is still unresolved.

So, is a system prompt all you need?

For removing the mechanical workflow in my setup, it was enough. I no longer needed an elaborate skill to prescribe the research sequence. That was a useful change.

For avoiding premature commitment and stopping, we are still working on it.

One possibility we discussed is separating research decisions from routine implementation. Let the main agent keep the objective, competing explanations, and unresolved questions in view. Let subagents handle bounded coding and experiment tasks, then return the evidence—including failures.

My suggestion was to keep the main context from filling up with the details of making one implementation work. A worker finishing its assignment would give the main agent something to evaluate, rather than become the end of the research session.

That is a proposed experiment, not a demonstrated fix. A main agent that delegates only DP variants would still have the same problem. And keeping the process running is separate from making its research decisions better.

The minimal prompt got us past “follow the procedure.” The question now is how to get past “make this idea work” and toward “find out which idea is worth pursuing.”

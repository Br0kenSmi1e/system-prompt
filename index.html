System Prompt
Is All You Need?

September 2026 · Draft

I was using sci-brain’s autoresearch workflow to look for new algorithms. It kept failing.

Failed experiments weren’t really what bothered me. Research fails all the time. What bothered me was how mechanical the whole thing felt: implement an idea, run the evaluation, write a report, hand the work back.

The agent was doing the tasks. I was still responsible for noticing what mattered and deciding where the research should go.

I put it this way:

“i feel like the agent is just following the skill mechanically. to me, it just wants to get the work done to throw back something to me, rather than being a curious researcher.”

That made me wonder whether the workflow was helping the agent investigate—or giving it a procedure to finish instead.

Why is the research still my job?

The problem became especially clear when I asked the agent to look into other AI research systems. Here’s my request and its reply. My message is unchanged; […] marks omitted text in the reply.

Me

could you do a survey? i believe a lot people have tried to use ai for research, how do they design the framework?

Agent

[…]

I recommend four angles:

  1. Framework landscape: minimal agents, fixed pipelines, multi-agent teams, and evolutionary search.
  2. Research memory: how systems retain evidence, failed attempts, hypotheses, and evolving understanding.
  3. Negative results: where agents become repetitive, produce unsupported claims, or need human intervention.
  4. Evaluation: whether reported gains demonstrate scientific discovery, benchmark optimization, or merely task completion.

[…]

Next: reply “all four,” or name the angles you want prioritized.

I had asked for findings. It gave me homework.

Those were reasonable search angles. The problem was making me choose before it did anything. I didn’t know which would be interesting. Finding that out was part of the work I wanted to delegate.

We traced the menu to an explicit instruction in the survey skill. In this case, the agent was following the rule—and that was exactly the problem. The workflow assigned a research decision to me that I wanted the agent to make.

This didn’t explain every failure in the earlier campaign. But it gave me a concrete reason to question the design: how many of our rules were helping the investigation, and how many were just making it wait for me?

What if I just let it think?

I liked the minimal design of pi. It made me wonder whether I could get the research behavior I wanted by changing the system prompt instead of building another elaborate skill.

My wording at the time was “free agent’s mind.” I believed Astra had the ability to research. Maybe I should give it the objective and the tools, then leave it room to decide whether to read, derive, code, experiment, or reconsider an assumption.

So we tried it. We replaced the procedural research workflow with a system prompt describing how to approach the problem. We kept a notebook, RESEARCH.md, for the objective and the understanding accumulated along the way.

The important change wasn’t the number of words. It was what the instructions tried to control: what the agent should care about, rather than which step it should perform next.

Of course, removing the procedure didn’t automatically give the agent good judgment. The next sessions made that clear.

Freedom wasn’t enough. We had to talk about taste.

My research problem was tensor-network contraction order: finding an order of operations that makes a large calculation cheaper. I wanted a competitive new algorithm with an idea worth understanding.

One session found engineering speedups. Another produced a careful study of how preprocessing could exclude good contraction orders. There were proofs, experiments, and sensible caveats. These weren’t empty reports.

But the agent kept treating useful supporting work as the result I had asked for. A faster implementation could help us search. A diagnostic could reveal a weakness. Neither, on its own, was the algorithmic contribution I wanted.

I called this a problem of “taste.” The agent could do the work, but its choice of what was worth doing didn’t match mine.

“Find something more interesting” wasn’t much of an instruction. We made the objective more explicit in the notebook: a substantively new contraction-order algorithm, competitive with strong existing methods. Diagnostics and implementation speedups were supporting work, not the objective.

The system prompt added a question to answer before substantial implementation:

Before substantial implementation, state the proposed algorithmic advance, why it matters, its assumptions, and expected scaling.

That didn’t tell the agent which algorithm to invent. It asked it to confront the difference between “I can implement this” and “this could advance the research.”

Keep the possibilities open.

Another pattern kept appearing: list several directions, pick one, and spend the rest of the session developing it. The agent could revise that one idea intelligently. The alternatives mostly stayed on the list.

I wanted an evolving investigation, not an opening brainstorm followed by commitment. Test enough to learn something, compare the possibilities, modify them, and let the results suggest new directions. Concentrate effort as the evidence improves—not just because one idea already has code.

We went back and forth on this until I found a way to express what I meant: be welcoming about adding ideas, strict about killing them, and active about changing them.

A weak first implementation shouldn’t eliminate an entire direction. Setting an idea aside isn’t the same as disproving it. And keeping possibilities open doesn’t require spending equal time on everything.

This became a central guideline:

Maintain an evolving pool of distinct ideas there. Welcome new directions and actively revise or combine ideas as you learn. Progressively compare and deepen promising candidates without committing prematurely.

The rest of that guideline asks for strong evidence before rejecting an idea. Again, the instruction was about how to judge the work, not a fixed sequence of experiments.

There were encouraging moments. A survey-first session proposed a symmetry-based exact planner, moving away from the previous line of work. Its reported advantage was narrow and novelty was unresolved, but it was a genuinely different direction.

Then it finished the study and stopped.

The original pattern hadn’t disappeared. The agent could do a better study and still treat that study as the end of the job. I wanted the result to inform its next decision: strengthen this idea, explore another, or revise the pool.

Our next proposed correction was:

Keep this result as a checkpoint, not the finish line.
Reassess and evolve the idea pool, then continue investigating.

That was still something to try, not a solved autonomy problem. But it expressed the distinction I kept coming back to: finishing an experiment is not necessarily finishing the research.

So, is a system prompt all you need?

I don’t know yet. This didn’t end with a breakthrough algorithm or a completely autonomous researcher.

What changed was how I approached the instructions. Instead of adding another stage whenever the agent disappointed me, I tried to identify the research judgment I wanted it to make: distinguish significance from correctness, keep alternatives alive, and use findings to decide what comes next.

Those expectations could live in the system prompt. The specific problem and what we learned could live in the notebook. The agent could choose the investigative path.

This isn’t proof that system prompts outperform skills. A skill can express the same principles, and a system prompt can be just as procedural. For me, changing the setup was a way to reconsider what I was asking the agent to own.

I still want constraints on evidence, resources, and safety. I don’t want to prescribe every ordinary research decision just because I can.

I want to give the agent a problem I don’t know how to solve—and have it help me figure out where to go, rather than ask me for the route.
